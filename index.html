Projects

Carbon Calculus

June 2025 – Present
Website (opens in a new tab)

Dr. Sarah Cooney recommended me for this role based on my contributions to her research team, and Simon Brooks, a PhD student in Sustainable Engineering at Villanova, hired me to build an open-source web tool presenting the findings of his Carbon Calculus research in an interactive, accessible format. I built it with JavaScript, React, Recharts, and Vite, backed by Supabase and deployed on Vercel. The tool models CO₂ reductions and lifecycle costs, computing NPV across a case study → scenario → project data hierarchy. It features dynamic visualizations (cumulative and stacked comparisons) and validated JSON import/export so users can compare scenarios. I meet weekly with Simon and Dr. Cooney to iteratively refine the design and functionality, incorporating feedback from Sustainable Engineering PhD students and faculty along the way. It's the first undergraduate project funded by Villanova's Strategic Initiative for Climate, Justice, and Sustainability (SICJS), and it is now publicly available at carboncalculus.com (opens in a new tab). As a Graduate Assistant, I now lead ongoing research extending its emissions and lifecycle cost models.

American Sign Language Classifier

August 2025 – December 2025
Code (opens in a new tab)

I trained a convolutional neural network in Python, PyTorch, and Scikit-Learn to classify 36 American Sign Language letters and digits from 2,515 images, reaching 95% accuracy on a held-out test set of 378 images after tuning and comparing 5 architectures. I analyzed per-class precision and recall to trace errors to visually similar signs (M vs. N, W vs. 6), and documented the full pipeline, from data preprocessing to model evaluation, in a public GitHub repository for end-to-end reproducibility.

Research

The Language of Over-Consumption: Insights from YouTube Haul Transcripts

January 2025 – August 2026
Undergraduate Research Assistant

This project continued my earlier #sheinhaul and #thrifthaul research with the same Computer Science team under Dr. Sarah Cooney, within Sustainable Human-Computer Interaction (SHCI). I built Python programs using the YouTube Transcript API to generate transcripts of #haul videos, and used Beautiful Soup to scrape related sustainability articles from newspaper databases. The team performed lexical analysis comparing #sheinhaul and #thrifthaul transcripts against the sustainability press coverage, and wrote up our findings for potential publication.

#AntiHaul: Creator Motivation and Anti-Consumption

May 2024 – December 2024
VURF Program & Undergraduate Research Assistant Presented Poster (opens in a new tab)

I started this work through Villanova's Undergraduate Research Fellowship (VURF) over the summer and continued it into a formal Undergraduate Research Assistant position for Fall 2024, under Dr. Sarah Cooney. This project examined the #antihaul trend on YouTube, an extension of my earlier #sheinhaul and #thrifthaul work, within the SHCI space. I wrote Python programs against YouTube's Public Data API to build a candidate dataset of roughly 1,000 videos, then manually reviewed each entry for relevance given the API's inconsistency. The team coded creator motivations against viewer engagement and found minimal correlation between the two. I designed and presented a poster on this work at Villanova's Undergraduate Research Symposium in November 2024, and again at the VU Women in Tech conference in January 2025.

#Haul: An Analysis of YouTube Discourse Surrounding Over-Consumption and Sustainability in Haul Videos

January 2024 – May 2024
MATCH Program Published Paper (opens in a new tab)

I joined this Computer Science research team under Dr. Sarah Cooney through Villanova's First-Year MATCH Program. The project, categorized under Sustainable Human-Computer Interaction (SHCI), examined the #haul trend on YouTube through the #sheinhaul and #thrifthaul lenses. I built Python programs against YouTube's Public Data API to collect and analyze roughly 1,000 videos across two subsets, one per hashtag, which the team then manually verified given the API's inconsistency. We coded each video, description, and comment section for pro- or anti-consumption themes and analyzed the results with a focus on sustainability across video-sharing platforms, applying data modeling, text analysis, and visualization techniques throughout. The resulting paper was accepted and published at NordiCHI 2024.

Non-Recursive Solution of the Tower of Hanoi

January 2024 – May 2024
Co-Master Program Published Paper (opens in a new tab) Website (opens in a new tab)

I joined a Mathematics research team under Dr. Alexander Diaz-Lopez through Villanova's Co-Master Program to explore the Tower of Hanoi's known most-efficient solution, aiming to derive an equation predicting which peg holds any given disk at any given move. As the only computer scientist on the team, I wrote Python simulations to verify our equation against millions of generated inputs before the team formalized the result through mathematical proof. We presented our work at the EPaDel conference at Albright College and at Villanova's Mathematics Symposium; I also built an informal, mobile-friendly website letting conference attendees enter any move number and see the resulting position of every disk in a 50-disk tower. The paper was published in Mathematics Magazine in July 2025.
